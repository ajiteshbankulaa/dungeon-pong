# What Returns — final editorial and play audit

5 November 2026 · Source checkpoint [a5c6163 on Poem](https://github.com/ajiteshbankulaa/dungeon-pong/commit/a5c616390c6c6cb7ba3b830abc8298321176ff63). This document proposes edits for the next build. The checkpoint is the baseline.

## The piece I think you have

On Tuesday, another person sends the ball where you can reach it. Later, their recorded paddle makes the same movement. In daylight your own hand still knows where to go. That gives *desiderium* a particular shape in this game: wanting one ordinary exchange with a person whose part you know by heart. The strongest ending is someone picking up their own cup while their hand still remembers the second one. They can live through this morning and want the other person back at the same time.

The assignment asks for one tiny game about a specific feeling, within a collection of three. Pong, one room, two colors, one short interruption, and three treatments of one score are enough. Keep the five returning room names as an internal structure. On screen, give each act a continuous sense of place. The repeated rhythm of opening panel → instructions → task → closing panel → Enter currently makes several passages feel like exercises annotated with verses. Change where the line lands before rewriting the line. Give speech to a person, a sound to a contact, and an observation to a hand that has just done something.

## What I checked

Read the assignment PDF, inspected the saved source and script, ran the 35 tests on the GitHub checkout, rendered 15 passages and six additional compositions, and compared them at a common scale. The smoke renders are illustrative frames; the script sets some modes directly. The full-route test separately exercises progression. Instrumenting that test gave 114 simulated seconds, including transitions but essentially no reading, exploration, or novice retries. That is a lower-context timing baseline, not a measured audience runtime. I also decoded all three shipped music tracks and examined their boundaries. Mix judgments below distinguish signal measurements from things that require ears in the room. Emotional interpretation still needs human players.

## The playable score, beat by beat

### Entry and 1. Tuesday / Serve

Start is quiet, and “You always let me serve first” immediately tells us something about both people. The wide room has two cups, a window, a table, and a machine. The player's first substantial action is actually approaching the machine and finding E. The partner is standing apart. Then the camera cuts to a huge court. It is possible to learn the controls without understanding who is doing the letting. The rendered title also runs close to the bright window edge.

Place the companion at the second control, hand still on the rim, while the player approaches. Frame both controls just once. Let the waiting hand be visible behind the opening line. Show the ball anchored beside the player's paddle, with the serve binding near it. On the first low return, lift the purple cuff early enough for the player to move in response. Keep “where I could reach” after the player has actually reached it. One generous exchange followed by one recognizably repeated cue may teach more than three similar returns. Test whether someone notices the courtesy without being told which paddle is generous.

### 2. Angle

“Show me the corner one” gives the corner a social purpose. “Again. I was watching your hand” is the most alive the companion sounds. The thin target, dotted court, and aim guide currently have to make the entire scene legible. At success the ball is cleared and the closing text is staged over the court. The player hears about a gaze they barely have a chance to see.

Keep the forgiving guide. Put a small gap and two corner ticks in the boundary, so the destination reads by shape even when colors wash out. At the successful shot, let the ball go through; allow a short picture of the purple figure leaning toward the player's control while the ball passes. Put “Again” there. Keep the player free to stay with the line. This becomes the memory the game will later fail to reproduce.

### 3. Between

The player just learned to aim past someone. Now they deliberately aim toward them. This is the first genuinely poetic reversal in the controls. The heading, receiving band, “their reach” label, opening explanation, and closing explanation compete to describe it. The actor catches the second placement and the passage finishes immediately; the player may never see that last ball come back despite the text saying it did.

Let the second easy return travel home. Show “Neither of us was keeping score” on that return. Keep one practical affordance at the far paddle. Try cutting “This time, I sent it straight to you” in a playtest. If the change of aim still reads, those words bought nothing. The pleasure should come from discovering that a technically worse competitive shot makes a better shared game.

### 4. Hold

Four exchanges make a phrase. The bus cue is tied to the second player contact. The window light follows elapsed passage time and the nearly opaque court sits over much of the window. An unclued player can hear a low tone and read a bus sentence without perceiving the decision that ties them together.

Use one event to drive the light across the wall and the arrival sound. Let both end; then let the companion return one more ball in the quieter room. That final return is the answer to “You could have gone.” Preserve the user's thought of staying for another exchange. A possible shorter closing is “Your bus went. / You served again,” but only use “served” if they actually serve. With the present action, “Your bus went. / You sent it back.” is accurate.

### 5. Again?

“One more?” followed by “I said yes” is small enough to feel ordinary. The present script requires three returns before arresting the next outward ball. In the scripted route this beat, including its transition, took longer than the sustained-rally passage. The invitation loses its immediacy.

Let pressing Space be the yes. Freeze that serve at the center seam. Leave the frozen shot behind “I said yes.” Keep the coat in the living scene if there is time for one inexpensive gesture: the companion lays it across the back of the machine and turns to the stick. This establishes a specific coat before the absence scene.

### Winter / the interruption

The hard silence, familiar character colors, and domestic cut are stronger than the car itself. A car foregrounded beside a crosswalk tends to imply cause; two people standing safely on the curb can also read as nothing happening. Meanwhile the figures are small enough that the newly lit cuff is easy to miss. The coat does move, though its position at rest and the distance between the pegs matter more than the polygon's amplitude.

Direction confirmed: viewers should understand the death; its cause stays uncertain. My first cut would keep the street as the narrator's last *ordinary recollection*. Show both cuffs together under one pool of light. Let a reflection pass across them without a vehicle converging on either person. Hard cut to the same home. Move certainty into a single domestic detail. Candidate line: “After the funeral / I hung my coat beside your peg.” Let the sleeve swing once into the empty place. Try this alongside a version with only “I hung mine up.” If viewers infer a breakup from the second version, use the funeral line. There is no need for a hospital, collision, scream, or additional exposition. Preserve the silence throughout pause and resume. Let the text wait for its reader if it cannot be read within the fixed visual beat.

### Restart / the quarter

“I fed the machine a quarter. / It gave me back Tuesday.” earns its leap because the player spends something small and gets something impossibly valuable in return. The coin view draws the window, figure, and machine, but drops some of the furniture that identifies the room as home. A stranger might still imagine a visit to a public arcade.

Keep the table and empty second control in the same positions during the insert. Make the quarter visible on the ledge in Tuesday. On E, let it disappear under the player's thumb and give it a brief mechanical click. The next familiar cuff motion should feel briefly, sincerely welcome. Keep both lines.

### 6. Reconstruction / Serve

The recording is now real: Tuesday's paddle positions and cuff samples are played back against a live ball. That is a material improvement over reactive ghost AI. The stored performance is a sequence from the captured serve, rather than a complete film of every ball in Tuesday. If paths diverge, timing and contact can drift. Where a legacy checkpoint lacks a recording, the fallback is a static paddle; the line about “that little lift” may have no counterpart. More subtly, the passage says “There. That little lift” before the player has served.

Move “There.” to the first recognizable movement. Delay “I knew where it was going” until the player has received it. Give missing recordings a short authored fallback using the same cue and generous return. Test entering from an older save and deliberately serving from an unusual height. A failed attempt must clearly reoffer the gesture.

### 7. Reconstruction / Angle

The saved angle is one of the strongest uses of real game data. The player can match the line and wait for the exact words the companion used last time. The guide and recorded line can be difficult to distinguish; a different color alone is insufficient. Increasing tolerance helps progression but can make the machine certify a visibly different path.

Draw the recording as short dashes and the live aim as one fine stroke. At generous acceptance, visually settle the shot into the saved path so success looks like success. Keep “I wait for you to say ‘again.’” Give the empty control the visual emphasis that the living person's glance received in Tuesday. The silence after this specific joke matters more than an explanatory line about simulation.

### 8. Reconstruction / Between

“Miss one for me” is probably the strongest new line. In Tuesday the narrator wanted the companion to reach the shot. Here the narrator would give anything for the little failure that proved someone was choosing how to play. The current closing, “You used to miss that one,” attaches the memory to a cooperative placement that the living companion was specifically required to catch twice. The actual miss we remember was the corner shot.

Keep the two placements and identical answers. Change the closing to “I liked your excuse.” It recalls the hand-watching scene without inventing a miss on this shot. Or test leaving the second perfect return unanswered by any closing text. Stop on the far paddle moving to the same spot, with time to notice the duplication.

### 9. Reconstruction / Hold

The player catches the blue-green path and lets the purple path travel. “I played my half. / Yours played without you” is legible in the rules. Sound currently weakens that division: the two opening cues fire together on launch, and the player's catch also triggers the hollow sound. It is hard to know which action produced which voice.

Give the player's note exactly at their catch. Place the recorded note at the far path's own visible contact or crossing, on its own clock. Give the two paths different shapes as well as inks. A player with sound off should still be able to see which part would have sounded even if they had put the controller down. No punishment for letting the purple part pass.

### 10. Reconstruction / Again? and walking away

“I wanted another game. / I wanted the quiet, too” allows incompatible wishes to coexist. Keep it. The replay prompt promises a point but re-enters the passage, including its opening text and a set of returns. A person testing the replay may feel they have undone progress. Walking away already fades the machine's volume in proportion to distance. Replaying after a partial retreat is worth checking: same-act music entry can leave the attenuated volume in place.

Offer one replayed exchange and return directly to the two choices. Keep the real bus independent of the stored point; currently the bus cue fires on its arrested crossing. Make the first step back subtract one layer of audible presence. Let a player change their mind. On a complete withdrawal, let one unaccompanied room sound bridge the dark screen and daylight. Morning arriving is enough; resist adding a conclusion about acceptance.

### 11. Morning / Serve

The player sees the machine turned off and touches it. Horizontal travel is now controlled. That fixes an important prior flaw. In a still frame the cleared dust is a thin, neat line, the hand is angular, and the reflected person is tiny against a large, flat pane. The instruction to release at the far edge teaches a hidden threshold as much as it teaches contact. Lifting early keeps the trace recoverable, a good property.

Keep the joystick and rim just inside the crop. Carry a distinctive corner of the room's window into the reflection so this glass belongs to that room. Make dust visible immediately around the contact rather than adding noise across the whole screen. Let a sufficiently intentional stroke earn “Dust on my thumb,” with a small smear on the lifted pad. Reserve crossing the whole width for Hold, where the distance means something.

### 12. Morning / Angle

“You wanted to see my hand. / Like this.” is the best example of gameplay and poetry meeting: a dead person once asked a living person to show them something, and much later they finally do it slowly. The implemented check accepts any stroke ending far enough right. A straight horizontal line earns “Like this” just as readily as the remembered corner. The rendered scene also resembles morning Serve almost exactly.

Use the actual Tuesday corner as a soft point of attention. A faint clean patch where the first trace ended or a slight framing shift will do. Angle the finger toward it; accept a broad area and allow an expressive imperfect line. No target ring, failure sound, or mathematical alignment. Start the line of dialogue on contact; let “Like this” appear where the hand comes to rest.

### 13. Morning / Between

The game moves into the room. Carry a cup, approach the table, place it, hear the clink, reach toward the shelf. The next hand movement is the poem. A shelf and second cup now give that reach a destination. But the carried cup uses a fixed horizontal offset, even when the player walks left, and placement interpolates to a fixed table point from anywhere inside a broad interaction radius. The hand and ceramic can visibly part company. A cup painted onto the morning shelf also needs a clean silhouette distinct from the prompt.

Anchor the cup handle to the same wrist used by the walking figure. On E ease the body the last short distance toward the tabletop. Release the ceramic once; only then turn toward the shelf. Let the hand stop a few centimeters short and fall. Keep “My hand went back for yours” if the gesture reads without it. If the gesture doesn't read, fix the movement before adding words. The ring must sit on the top plane and survive the walk back.

### 14. Morning / Hold

This is the decisive scene. The player finishes a trace, lifts, and has 1.5 seconds in which to prepare for a return. Yet the draw code puts the hand at the near edge whenever the phase is no longer playing. During the wait, vertical movement works, but horizontal movement back to receiving position is supplied by the renderer. A still player appears to have made the very gesture the poem claims. The automated test checks vertical mobility and the clock; it cannot distinguish reflex from compliance.

Keep the lifted hand exactly where the player leaves it. Allow free movement with contact up. Draw no new dust under a lifted hand. If the player moves back, let “My hand was ready / before I remembered” land after that movement. If they choose to stay still, keep the timing generous and use a truthful alternative such as “I waited there.” Both choices finish. Do not instruct them to wait for an answer. This is the first fix I would playtest.

### 15. Morning / Again?

The final invitation can be answered with another trace or by leaving. Both lead to the same coda. That is generous: replaying does not mean failure to grieve correctly. The verb “lift” is awkward when the hand has not yet touched the screen. Keyboard and controller also have different conditions on when leaving is accepted.

Use “draw again” and “leave the glass.” Make both inputs available from the same idle state. Allow time, with no new verse and no score. Carry the hand or camera away from the glass to the physical room. The player's last choice is allowed to remain private.

### Coda, final card, replay

Walk to the cup placed earlier. Pick it up. “Then I picked up mine. / It was still warm.” Keep this. The interval spent back at the machine gives “still” its meaning. A fixed sprite offset and instant pickup remain the weakest details. The proximity label appears at a slightly more generous distance than the pickup action actually accepts. That can create one last, particularly damaging dead keypress.

Use one proximity predicate for label and input. Animate the handle settling into the hand. Keep the ring visible next to the held cup. Let the chord sound once after the player reads “warm.” Check in headphones whether the looping morning music competes with the final chord on the lingering title. Put the replay opening in the same framing as the first serve: “I remember you letting me serve first.” Naming the memory once, here, completes the poem.

## Sound, image, controls, and evidence across scenes

- **Score.** The shipped movements decode to 90, 96, and 87 seconds. Tuesday's hit notes follow its changing harmony. Preserve that relationship. The motor-like second movement and more spacious third movement already offer substantial material. One well-placed gap after a missing response will buy more than another composition.
- **Loop seams.** Morning's last half-second decoded as digital silence at the analysis floor; its opening half-second averaged approximately −34 dBFS. Tuesday's final-to-first mono sample step was about 0.028 full scale. These indicate where to listen, not a verdict that a click or gap is audible. Audition ten seconds on either side of each *actual playback* boundary, slow-reader loops, mute/resume, withdrawal, and the final card. If morning audibly restarts, overlap a sustained piano tail or a quiet room resonance beneath its opening. Keep the rupture's silence intentional.
- **Visual continuity.** The common-scale sheet shows the first five courts and most of the next five differentiated mainly by objective overlays and palette. Morning's four glass pictures are almost interchangeable. Make each distinction arise from behavior: waiting, looking aside, hitting within reach, catching perfectly, preparing for silence. Spend the art budget on the cuff, thumb, cup handle, and peg. Test from the back of a classroom as well as at full window size.
- **Reading.** Keep Enter discoverable on an actual reading pause. Preserve one strong image behind each line. Avoid simultaneously printing a poem about a shot, an objective describing the shot, and a tutorial restating the objective. Show bindings before input is needed; reveal narrative after a gesture has earned it. No forced text deadlines.
- **Confirmed input defects.** In the audited checkout, releasing Space while paused at the end of a morning stroke advanced to waiting. Pressing the controller's secondary button while paused on the last glass scene entered the coda. Pause should capture input before gameplay release and leave handlers. On focus return, require fresh contact. Also verify silence survives resuming on the hook screen, whose Enter-resume route currently calls score entry.
- **Feedback.** Early-release feedback and glass controls share the same bottom text position. Separate them or let a recovery message temporarily replace the hint. The court feedback also substitutes for missing visual clarity; make retry instructions identify the player's next action. Show bindings appropriate to the active input device.
- **Distribution.** The commit saves source, build recipe, and assets. Build products, personal saves, and generated previews were excluded from this checkpoint. Verify the Windows folder launches after extraction with its bundled dependencies, including on another machine. The assignment's final submission is the Itch page for all three poems. This review covers Week 2 only.
